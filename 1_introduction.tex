\section{Introduction}\label{intro}

Open Source Software (OSS) has become a cornerstone of modern
software development, fostering innovation, knowledge exchange, and
the widespread adoption of software tools and libraries.  The
licensing of OSS projects dictates how software can be used,
modified, and distributed. Not all OSS participants follow or are
aware of the ramifications licensing~\cite{}. For example, that
their code is under exclusive copyright by default if no license is specified. 
The lack of knowlede, and/or, possibly, the lack of enforcement,
results in reuse of unlicences code in context where license
incompatibilities are likely. For example, with the emergence of
LLMs, the massive source code training data does
include in large proprtion 
code with no license~\cite{kocetkov2022stack,sc2}.

Our aim is to better understand the actual practice of license
selection, including the choice of no license, specifically because
copying no-license code is likely to result in license violation and
because other studies have found that much of copied code even in
large and popular projects is copied from small and inactive
projects~\cite{jahanshahi2024beyond} that tend to have no license. 
The problemmay be propageted further, as the active and popular
projects are often upstream dependencies for numerous projects
downstream. Hens if such inapropriate use is detected, it can affect
a very large part of OSS ecosystem, not unlike what happend with
LEFTPAD. 


Prior work found relativel low frequency of repositories with no
license (e.g. X\% in ~\cite{}, Y\% in Z), that, as we demonstrate,
greatly underestimates the proportion of projects with no license
due to heavily filtering based on size, maturity, popularity, and
activity.

What does ``more comprehensively identify licenses'' mean? Why
exactly do we need it? Perhaps: Projects with uncommon lincenses
or slight variations in common licenses tend to be ignored in
prior studies, e.g,~\cite{a,b,c}. Yet, as we show, these ignored

In OSS, decision often are made based on precieved supply chain
concers, such as chosing to participate in a popular project,
expecting to have large number of direct or downstream users 
Moreover, license choice, much like technology
selection~\cite{choice23,ma2020methodology}, often reflects
strategic decisions by project maintainers to balance openness,
protection, and control over their codebases.

License choice may also be influenced by technical factors (e.g.,
licenses used by dependent frameworks), social dynamics (e.g.,
preferences of collaborators), or simple awareness, such as the
popularity of certain licenses. 

Previous research has largely focused on cataloging license usage
through descriptive statistics, highlighting the distribution of
licenses across projects.  While some studies have explored
developer intentions and goals in license selection using survey
data~\cite{sen2008determinants}, these approaches tend to emphasize
subjective factors.  However, they have yet to quantitatively model
how project-specific contexts—such as project size, programming
language, or technical characteristics—affect license choice using
robust statistical methods.

This study addresses these gaps by analyzing a large-scale dataset
of open-source projects from the World of Code (WoC), offering a
more comprehensive perspective on OSS licensing across a wide range
of projects.  Unlike previous research that concentrated on smaller
datasets or specific package managers, this broader dataset enables
the identification of larger trends, such as the high prevalence of
unlicensed projects, and provides a more complete understanding of
license usage across the OSS ecosystem.  The broader scope also
allows for the capture of patterns that might have been missed in
earlier, smaller-scale analyses.

Furthermore, while raw frequencies provide a basic snapshot of
license usage, they fail to account for project-specific factors
that can influence license selection.  By employing a multinomial
regression model and a stratified sampling approach, this study
investigates how contextual factors such as project size, age, and
programming language impact license choice.  This method reduces
biases that arise from focusing solely on popular projects or
package managers, while also preventing the overamplification of
small projects' effects.  As a result, it offers deeper insights
into the relationships between project attributes and license
selection, enabling a more nuanced understanding of licensing
behavior that descriptive statistics alone cannot provide.

To achieve these goals, we aim to develop a model that (a)
comprehensively accounts for all license types, including minor and
non-semantic variations, (b) is applied to a dataset approximating
the full scale of the OSS ecosystem, and (c) incorporates key
contextual factors that may influence license selection priorities
among projects and contributors.

The core questions our research seeks to answer are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1}: What are the types and prevalence of licenses across the entire open source software landscape?
    \item \textbf{RQ2}: How do the characteristics of a project influence the choice of its license?   
\end{itemize}

We analyzed the types and prevalence of licenses across more than
131 million OSS projects indexed by WoC, finding that 83\% of these
projects lack a formal license.  This absence poses significant
legal uncertainties for both developers and users.  Among the
licensed projects, the MIT license was the most commonly used,
accounting for 65\% of all licensed projects, followed by the Apache
2.0 and ISC licenses.  Although permissive licenses dominate
overall, copyleft licenses like the GPL exhibited high retention
rates, reflecting a strong commitment to software freedom within
certain communities.

A comparison with previous studies highlights notable differences,
primarily due to variations in dataset size and methodology.  Our
study, based on a larger dataset, reveals a higher percentage of
unlicensed projects than previously reported, underscoring the need
to prioritize license adoption to mitigate legal risks in the OSS
ecosystem.

We further investigated whether project characteristics influence
license selection in OSS projects using a multinomial regression
model.  Key factors such as the number of commits, project age, and
file count were found to significantly affect license choice.
Projects with more commits and older projects tended to adopt
copyleft licenses, whereas permissive licenses were more commonly
chosen by newer projects and those with more contributors.

Additionally, projects with a larger number of files were more
likely to adopt conditional open or weak copyleft licenses, while
those with fewer files favored permissive licenses.  The choice of
programming language also played a critical role, with languages
such as JavaScript and Go leaning toward permissive licenses, while
C/C++ and Perl showed higher adoption of copyleft licenses.
