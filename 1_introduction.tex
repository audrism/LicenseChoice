\section{Introduction}\label{intro}

Open Source Software (OSS) has become a cornerstone of modern
software development, fostering innovation, knowledge exchange, and
the widespread adoption of software tools and libraries.  The
licensing of OSS projects dictates how software can be used,
modified, and distributed. Not all open source software (OSS) 
participants are aware of or follow the ramifications of 
licensing.~\cite{almeida2017software}. For example, that
their code is under exclusive copyright by default if no license is specified. 
The lack of knowledge, and/or, possibly, the lack of enforcement,
results in reuse of unlicensed code in context where license
incompatibilities are likely. For example, with the rise of 
large language models (LLMs), a significant portion of the 
massive source code used for training consists of code without any license.~\cite{kocetkov2022stack,sc2}.

Our aim is to better understand the actual practice of license
selection, including the choice of no license, specifically because
copying no-license code is likely to result in license violation and
because other studies have found that much of copied code even in
large and popular projects is copied from small and inactive
projects~\cite{jahanshahi2024beyond} that tend to have no license. 
The problem may be propagated further, as the active and popular
projects are often upstream dependencies for numerous projects
downstream. Hens if such inappropriate use is detected, it can affect
a very large part of OSS ecosystem, not unlike what happened with
LEFTPAD. 

Prior work found relativel low frequency of repositories with no
license (e.g. X\% in ~\cite{}, Y\% in Z), that, as we demonstrate,
greatly underestimates the proportion of projects with no license
due to heavily filtering based on size, maturity, popularity, and
activity.

In OSS, any decision is often are made based on percieved supply
chain concers, such as chosing to participate in a project that will
become popular, hoping to have large number of direct or downstream
users, selecting upstream dependencies that appear healthy,
responsive, and active~\cite{choice23,ma2020methodology} and so on.
License choice has clear and far reaching effects on the supply
chains in terms of attracting and retaining participants, having
downstream dependents or users, and, of course on ways the code may
be (re-)used. Despite that we are not aware of prior studies that model
license choice nor any comprehensive theories that would explain it.

Our aim is, therefore, to establish and test a preliminary theory
that helps explain license choice in OSS software. The theory could
help explain some of the reasons why a large fraction of projects
have no license, why some license violations appear to be tolerated,
such as use of unlicensed code, what are factors that may come into
play when a developer (or an organization) chose a license.  Such
theory can potentially lead to better tools that help developers
chose the most suitable license according to their objectives,
increase the proportion of projects with a license (hence spurring
innovation that is presently inhibiting actors who do pay attention
to licensing terms from using unlicensed code), and, more generally,
reduce risks arising from license incompatibilities in OSS supply
chain.

To accomplish our goals we start from reviewing litureture in areas
that may help us formulate a preliminary theory, such as OSS developer
motivation, company involvement in OSS, existing theories on how
technology and libraries are selected and spread, as well as
literature on OSS licensing. 

We pay particular attention to phenomena that could be measured at
scale in order to make our theory easily testable. We then engage in
an effort to identify licenses and license types for nearly entire
OSS in order to capture not only the most popular but also include
as many projects with no license that tend to be small and
inactive. In particular, unlike most prior work, e.g,~\cite{a,b,c}., we try not to
ignore projects with rare types of licenses or slight variations in
license text because, as we show, these ignored licenses ...whatever
problem happens if we exclude them from consideration...

Once we establish this lage curated list of licences and projects,
we use our preliminary theory to operationalize key factors where
theory predicts a certain effect on the choice. We first test
if certain types of licenses have longer retention rates based
on predictions of our theory.  
We then employ a multinomial regression model and a stratified
sampling approach, to test which of the theory-predicted contextual
factors impact license choice. 

Our contributions include (a) a preliminary theory of license choice,
(b) a comprehensively account for almost all license types, including minor and
non-semantic variations on a nearly full scale of the OSS ecosystem,
(c) theory-derived predictors of license choice, and (d)
an evaluation of the preliminary theory using a multinomial model of
license choice.

Our analysis of the types and prevalence of licenses across more
than 131 million OSS projects, finds that 83\% of these projects
lack a formal license. Among the licensed projects, the MIT license
was the most commonly used, accounting for 65\% of all licensed
projects, followed by the Apache 2.0 and ISC licenses.  Although
permissive licenses dominate overall, copyleft licenses like the GPL
exhibited high retention rates, reflecting a strong commitment to
software freedom within certain communities. In comparison to prior
studies we find *four-fold* (check) larger prevalence of unlicennsed
code.

Our theory-derived project characteristics that should influence
license selection in OSS projects were tested using a multinomial regression
model. As predicted, the number of commits, project age, and
file count were found to significantly affect license choice.
Projects with more commits and older projects tended to adopt
copyleft licenses, whereas permissive licenses were more commonly
chosen by newer projects and those with more contributors.

Additionally, projects with a larger number of files were more
likely to adopt conditional open or weak copyleft licenses, while
those with fewer files favored permissive licenses. The choice of
programming language also played a critical role, with languages
such as JavaScript and Go leaning toward permissive licenses, while
C/C++ and Perl showed higher adoption of copyleft licenses.
