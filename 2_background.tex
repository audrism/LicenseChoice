\section{Related Work and Contributions}

\subsection{Related Work}

To model licence choice, several strands of prior research may be relevant.
First is the motivation of individuals~\cite{von2012carrots} and businesses~\cite{Harhoff02} to participate in OSS.
Second, technical, social, and other factors that either constraint certain choices in software development or make them more convenient~\cite{ma2020methodology}.
Third, are the specific plans and objectives of the project, and how each license choice may advance and hinder these goals~\cite{sen2008determinants}.

\subsubsection{Motivation}
A literature review in \cite{von2012carrots} categorizes OSS participant motivations into ideology, altruism, kinship, fun, reputation, reciprocity, learning, own-use, career, and pay.
Ideology and reciprocity are likely to favor copyleft licenses, which require code sharing, while altruism may lean toward more permissive licenses.

Motivations for commercial involvement may vary even if they generally fall under the umbrella of profit maximization~\cite{Harhoff02}.
Studies on open and gated software communities have argued that firms may face challenges when attempting to create hybrid arrangements that balance community-based value creation with private value appropriation \cite{Shah06}.
However, community sponsorship and licensing may address complementary developer motivations, such that the influence of licensing on development activity is tied to the nature of the organizational sponsor.
For example, ``The presence of a nonmarket\footnote{
Authors use the term ``nonmarket'' to exclude for-profit organizations.
} 
sponsor may alleviate concerns as to the project’s future in the same way that a restrictive license would, in the sense that the restrictive license is not perceived as necessary to protect the developers’ interests''~\cite{stewart2006impacts}.

A study by~\cite{fershtman2007open} found that restrictive licenses attracted more contributors per project, whereas non-restrictive licenses led to higher output per contributor.
Additionally,~\cite{economicos02} discuss the advantages for firms releasing proprietary code and how less restrictive licenses can facilitate bundling proprietary and open-source code.

Motivation of commercial sponsors may also vary.
For example, a study of four firms involved in OSS identified three strategies that firms used to engage with OSS communities~\cite{dahlander2008firms}:
accessing development in the community in order to extend their resource base;
aligning their strategy with the work in the community; 
and assimilating communities in order to integrate and share results.
Study by~\cite{wagstrom2010impact,zhou2016inflow} identified two types of commercial involvement from analyzing GNOME and Eclipse projetcs:
community-focused companies that build a vibrant community (e.g., GNOME) and monetize through services, and product-focused companies that rely on product revenues.
Each model may necessitate different licensing strategies.

Since motivation of contributors and types of commercial sponsorship are not easy (or possible) to operationalize at scale, the key takeaway for modeling is that larger (in terms of contributors) are more likely to adopt restrictive licensing~\cite{fershtman2007open}.

\subsubsection{Social and Technical Choice}
Licenses, like other technologies and practices, spread. 
Works by~\cite{ma2020methodology,choice23} used theory of social contagion~\cite{shoroye2015exploring} to estimate the impact of exposure, susceptibility, and infectiousness on developer choice of packages.
They find that exposure measured via a larger number of overall deployments at the time of choice had a large positive effect on package selection.
We expect a similar pattern with license selection:
developers who are indifferent or unaware of the differences between licenses will gravitate toward those they have encountered before. 
Developers also tended to choose packages compatible with technologies they were already using (technical compatibility) and were more likely to select what their collaborators had used (social compatibility).
We expect a similar pattern for license choice, i.e., developers will likely select the same or a compatible license for future projects and will often choose a license already used by their collaborators.

\subsubsection{Project Needs}
Developers may choose licenses based on their preferences for the future use of the software.
Research by Sen, Subramaniam, and Nelson~\cite{sen2008determinants} shows that developers’ motivations, such as a desire for widespread adoption or strong copyleft enforcement, play a key role in license selection.

This choice can also significantly influence a project's development trajectory.
Colazo and Fang~\cite{colazo2009impact} examined how licenses, especially copyleft licenses, affect developer contributions and engagement.
Their study found that copyleft licenses often promote greater permanence and long-term involvement in projects.

Additionally, OSS licensing is crucial for business models that depend on open source software.
Onetti and Verma~\cite{onetti2009open} argued that some companies initially adopt open source licenses to build a community around their software but later adjust their licensing strategies to align with commercial interests.

Wu et al.~\cite{wu2024large} conducted a large-scale empirical study on open source license usage, providing insights into the practices and challenges faced by developers.
Their findings highlight the complexity of license compliance and the frequent misunderstandings and misuse of licenses in large projects.

Cui et al.~\cite{cui2023empirical} investigated license conflicts in free and open source software, uncovering the prevalence and impact of such conflicts.
They created a tool, DIKE, to detect license conflicts and examined over 16,000 popular open source projects, finding license conflicts in more than 25\% of them.
Their study showed that license conflicts often arise from misunderstandings of license terms and the complexities of multi-license environments.

Almeida et al.~\cite{almeida2017software} investigated whether software developers understand open source licenses.
Their study revealed significant gaps in knowledge, with many developers unable to correctly interpret license terms, leading to non-compliance and legal risks.

Multiple studies have shown that the declared license of a project cannot always be trusted ~\cite{german2010understanding, reid2023applying, wolter2023open}.
Wolter et al.~\cite{wolter2023open} analyzed open source license inconsistencies on GitHub.
They found that the license information declared at the top level of a project did not fully disclose all of the licenses found in the project in about 50\% of the projects studied.
Their study revealed widespread inconsistencies and provided insights into common types of licensing issues encountered, stressing the need for better education and automated tools to ensure compliance.

Xu et al.~\cite{xu2023lidetector, xu2023liresolver} made significant contributions with two notable papers.
In ``Lidetector'', they introduced automated methods for detecting license incompatibilities, helping developers identify potential legal issues early in the development process.
They used the tool to conduct an empirical study of 1,846 projects and found that over 70\% contained incompatibilities.
Their second paper, ``LiResolver'', proposed practical solutions to resolve detected incompatibilities, emphasizing the importance of tools that assist in managing license compliance.

\subsection{Contributions}

The literature on open source software licenses highlights the complexity and importance of licensing decisions within the software development ecosystem.
Recent studies have examined the dynamics of license selection across various programming languages, shedding light on how different developer communities approach license adoption and enforcement.
While foundational works established the basic principles, contemporary research emphasizes the unique challenges posed by the specific requirements of different languages and development environments.
Our contribution provides a data-driven analysis of these dynamics, offering a scalable framework to identify and evaluate license selection trends across programming languages.
This research fills a critical gap in understanding how language-specific factors affect license compliance, enabling developers to make more informed choices as they navigate the complexities of open source software licensing.
Specifically, our study contributions are as follows:

\subsubsection{Comprehensive Identification of Licenses} 
Most research, such as the work by Wu et al.~\cite{wu2024large} and Xu et al.~\cite{xu2023lidetector}, primarily depends on explicit license declarations found in metadata files.
Others, like Feng et al.~\cite{feng2019open}, apply static analysis on binaries to detect embedded license texts.
However, these methods may overlook licenses that are not clearly stated or are stored in unconventional directories.
In contrast, this study utilizes a dataset from Jahanshahi et al.~\cite{jahanshahi2024license}, which adopts a more comprehensive approach by scanning the entire OSS ecosystem for any files containing ``license'' in their filepath.
This method captures not only standard license files but also other files potentially holding licensing details, ensuring that no relevant license information is missed.

\subsubsection{Scale and Scope of Analysis} 
Previous works often limit their scope to specific platforms (e.g., GitHub), a few package manager environments (e.g., NPM), or types of licenses (e.g., OSI-approved licenses), lacking a comprehensive, large-scale approach to detecting and analyzing licenses.
Our study expands this scope by analyzing the entire open source landscape, ensuring a more comprehensive cross-platform understanding of licensing practices.
