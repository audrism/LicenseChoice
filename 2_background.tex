\section{Related Work and Contributions}

\subsection{Related Work}

To model licence choice, several strands of prior research may be relevant.
First is the motivation of individuals~\cite{von2012carrots} and businesses~\cite{Harhoff02} to participate in OSS.
Second, technical, social, and other factors that either constraint certain choices in software development or make them more convenient~\cite{ma2020methodology}.
Third, are the legal implications and the need to manage compliance with licenses in open-source software.
Fourth are the specific plans and objectives of the project, and how each license choice may advance and hinder these goals~\cite{sen2008determinants}.

\subsubsection{Motivation}



License choice may also be influenced by technical factors (e.g.,
licenses used by dependent frameworks), social dynamics (e.g.,
preferences of collaborators), or simple awareness, such as the
popularity of certain licenses. 

Moreover, license choice, much like technology selection~\cite{},
may reflect strategic decisions by project maintainers to balance
openness, protection, and control over their codebases.


Previous research has largely focused on cataloging license usage
through descriptive statistics, highlighting the distribution of
licenses across projects.  While some studies have explored
developer intentions and goals in license selection using survey
data~\cite{sen2008determinants}, these approaches tend to emphasize
subjective factors.  However, they have yet to quantitatively model
how project-specific contexts—such as project size, programming
language, or technical characteristics—affect license choice using
robust statistical methods.


A literature review by von Krogh and von Hippel~\cite{von2012carrots} categorizes OSS participant motivations into ideology, altruism, kinship, fun, reputation, reciprocity, learning, own-use, career, and pay.
These motivations can influence the type of licenses contributors prefer.
For instance, ideology and reciprocity are likely to favor copyleft licenses, which require code sharing, while altruism may lean toward more permissive licenses.

In the case of commercial involvement, motivations may diverge from individual contributors, even if they generally align with profit maximization~\cite{Harhoff02}.
Studies of open and gated software communities highlight the difficulties firms face in balancing community-based value creation with private value appropriation~\cite{Shah06}.
However, community sponsorship and licensing strategies can address these tensions.
For instance, restrictive licenses can ensure long-term control over contributions, whereas nonmarket\footnote{
The term ``nonmarket'' excludes for-profit organizations.
} sponsors might alleviate concerns about the project’s sustainability without the need for restrictive licenses~\cite{stewart2006impacts}.
This highlights how the nature of the sponsor—market or nonmarket—may influence licensing strategies and developer motivations.

Empirical studies further emphasize the connection between license choice and project structure.
Fershtman and Gandal~\cite{fershtman2007open} found that restrictive licenses attract more contributors per project, while permissive licenses lead to higher productivity per contributor.
Similarly, research into firm involvement in OSS shows that licensing choices may be driven by strategic goals.
For instance, firms may adopt permissive licenses to facilitate bundling proprietary and open-source code~\cite{economicos02}.
Dahlander and Magnusson~\cite{dahlander2008firms} identified three strategies firms use to engage with OSS communities: (1) accessing community development to extend resources, (2) aligning their strategy with community efforts, and (3) assimilating the community to integrate and share outcomes.

Different commercial models necessitate distinct licensing strategies.
Wagstrom et al.~\cite{wagstrom2010impact} and Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2016inflow} identified two types of firm engagement:
community-focused firms (e.g., GNOME), which prioritize building vibrant OSS communities and monetize through services, and product-focused firms (e.g., Eclipse), which rely on product revenues.
The community-focused model tends to favor more permissive licenses, encouraging broad participation, while product-focused firms may lean toward restrictive licenses to maintain control over proprietary product integration.

Given the complexity of operationalizing motivations and sponsorship types at scale, a practical takeaway for modeling is that larger projects (in terms of contributor count) are more likely to adopt restrictive licenses~\cite{fershtman2007open}.
Projects with more contributors benefit from diverse expertise and collaboration, often resulting in higher-quality contributions~\cite{crowston2005social}.
This dynamic can influence license choice, as larger projects may prefer licenses that promote openness and fairness or, alternatively, restrictive licenses to manage contributions and maintain control.

Another key factor is diversity in OSS projects.
Research shows that teams with greater diversity are correlated with higher levels of innovation and project quality~\cite{vasilescu2015gender}.
Projects with more diverse teams may favor licenses that ensure inclusivity and protect all contributors equitably.
This reflects a desire to foster security, fairness, and respect for individual contributions, which may lead such projects to adopt licenses emphasizing community governance and equitable protection~\cite{terrell2017gender}.

While diversity encompasses several dimensions (e.g., ethnicity, experience), uniformly operationalizing these across multiple dimensions is not feasible due to the lack of consistent data availability.
Therefore, we use the number of female authors as a proxy for diversity in this study.

In summary, we hypothesize that:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis (H1)}: The total number of authors, core authors\footnote{Those responsible for 80\%+ of the commits}, as well as the gender composition of contributors (female and male authors), influence the license choice in a project.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Social and Technical Choice}

Licenses, like other technologies and practices, spread through communities.
Prior studies, such as those by Ma et al.~\cite{ma2020methodology} and others~\cite{choice23}, used the theory of social contagion~\cite{shoroye2015exploring} to estimate the impact of exposure, susceptibility, and infectiousness on developer choices regarding package selection.
These studies found that exposure measured by the number of overall deployments at the time of choice, had a significant positive effect on package selection.

We expect a similar pattern for license selection.
Developers who are indifferent or unaware of the differences between licenses are likely to gravitate toward those they have encountered before.
Just as developers tend to choose packages that are technically compatible with their existing technology stack and socially aligned with their collaborators' choices, we anticipate a similar behavior in license choice.
Developers are likely to select a license compatible with their current projects or one already used by their collaborators.

The choice of programming language also influences license selection due to the culture and practices embedded within specific developer communities.
For example, languages such as Python and JavaScript which are commonly used in web development, often favor permissive licenses like MIT or Apache, which emphasize reuse and flexibility~\cite{lerner2005economics}.
In contrast, languages such as C or C++, which are dominant in systems programming, tend to be linked with copyleft licenses like GPL, prioritizing keeping derivative works open~\cite{fitzgerald2006transformation}.
The language captures the influence of community norms and technical compatibility on license selection.

The number of forks, stars, and community size associated with a project can also impact license decisions.
Tsay et al.~\cite{tsay2014influence} found that projects with a large number of forks are perceived as more valuable and trustworthy by the community.
Projects aiming to encourage collaboration and reuse to maintain this momentum may favor permissive licenses.
Similarly, Borges et al.~\cite{borges2018s} suggest that the number of stars can signal community approval, which might influence license choice by aligning with a project's goals—whether aiming for rapid growth with permissive licenses or protection of the codebase with restrictive licenses.

Furthermore, the number of upstream and downstream projects a project interacts with can play a crucial role in license selection.
Projects may adopt the same license as their upstream counterparts to ensure compatibility and seamless integration~\cite{gousios2014exploratory}.
Conversely, developers working on downstream projects may prefer more restrictive licenses, such as GPL, to safeguard their contributions from misuse and to protect developers' rights~\cite{german2009license}.

The decision to count upstream and downstream projects is motivated by the need to quantify a project's external interactions.
While analyzing the specific licenses of these projects would provide more detailed insights, counting them offers a practical approach to estimate the complexity and potential challenges related to license alignment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis (H2)}: The programming language, number of forks, stars, and upstream/downstream projects will affect the license choice decision in a project.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Legal and Regulatory Context}

Open-source software licenses serve not only as technical choices but also as legal instruments defining the rights and obligations of developers and users.
The selection of a license carries significant implications for intellectual property, liability, and compliance with copyright laws.

Wu et al.~\cite{wu2024large} conducted a large-scale empirical study revealing the complexities of license compliance and the frequent misunderstandings developers face, especially in large projects.
Similarly, Cui et al.~\cite{cui2023empirical} found that over 25\% of the 16,000 open-source projects they analyzed contained license conflicts, often due to misunderstandings of license terms.
Their tool, DIKE, helps detect such conflicts, underscoring the challenges of managing multiple licenses within a project.

Almeida et al.~\cite{almeida2017software} further emphasized the widespread difficulty developers have in correctly interpreting licenses, which can lead to non-compliance and legal risks.
These findings highlight the need for better education and tools to support effective license management.

Multiple studies have shown that declared project licenses are often inaccurate or incomplete~\cite{german2010understanding, reid2023applying, wolter2023open}.
Wolter et al.~\cite{wolter2023open} found that nearly 50\% of open-source projects on GitHub exhibited inconsistencies between the declared top-level license and the actual licenses used within the codebase.

Xu et al.~\cite{xu2023lidetector, xu2023liresolver} contributed to addressing these challenges with tools like Lidetector, which detects license incompatibilities, revealing that over 70\% of projects studied had such issues.
Their follow-up tool, LiResolver, provides solutions for resolving these incompatibilities, demonstrating the critical role of automated tools in managing license compliance.

Regulatory frameworks, such as the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and U.S. export control laws, add further complexity to licensing decisions, particularly in international and data-sensitive projects.
These regulations can influence license choices by introducing additional compliance challenges.

Delays in adopting a license often reflect the need for careful legal deliberation to ensure compliance with intellectual property laws and other legal frameworks.
Additionally, projects that interact with upstream components may prioritize selecting legally aligned licenses to facilitate integration, while downstream projects may adopt more restrictive licenses to protect their contributions~\cite{cui2023empirical, xu2023lidetector}.

Furthermore, larger projects, especially those with many contributors, are more likely to face legal challenges regarding intellectual property, contributor agreements, and international regulations.
In such cases, delayed license adoption may reflect careful legal evaluation aimed at mitigating risks~\cite{fershtman2007open}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis (H3)}: The license adoption delay, the number of upstream/downstream projects, particularly in terms of ensuring license compatibility and legal compliance, and the number of contributors will affect the license choice due to legal considerations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Project Needs}

While legal compliance sets essential boundaries, the specific needs and objectives of a project further influence the selection of an appropriate license.

Developers often choose licenses based on their preferences for the future use of the software. 
Research by Sen, Subramaniam, and Nelson~\cite{sen2008determinants} highlights that developers' motivations—such as a desire for widespread adoption or strong copyleft enforcement—play a significant role in license selection.

The choice of license can significantly influence a project’s development trajectory.
Colazo and Fang~\cite{colazo2009impact} found that copyleft licenses promote greater permanence and long-term involvement in projects, affecting both developer contributions and engagement.

Additionally, open-source licensing is crucial for business models that depend on open-source software.
Onetti and Verma~\cite{onetti2009open} argue that some companies initially adopt open-source licenses to build a community around their software but may later adjust their licensing strategies to align with evolving commercial interests.

The number of commits is a key indicator of project activity and maintenance. 
Koch~\cite{koch2002effort} suggests that projects with frequent commits tend to have more active development cycles, making them more reliable and attractive for reuse.
As project activity increases, maintainers may select licenses that either encourage contributions or protect the evolving codebase from potential misuse, balancing the needs for stability and control.

The number of files in a project reflects its complexity and modularity.
Mockus~\cite{mockus2007large} observed that larger projects with more files are more likely to offer reusable components, making license choice critical for balancing reuse and intellectual property protection.
Bird et al.~\cite{bird2009putting} further suggested that projects with more files tend to have modular structures, which encourage community engagement and adoption, potentially favoring more permissive licenses.

Time-based metrics-such as the earliest and latest commits, active months—provide insights into a project’s maturity and stability.
Capiluppi et al.~\cite{capiluppi2003characteristics} noted that older, more mature projects are often seen as more reliable, which could influence the choice of licenses that support long-term sustainability, such as copyleft licenses.
On the other hand, newer projects may opt for permissive licenses to facilitate rapid contributions during active development phases.

Burstiness, as a proxy for fluctuating activity, was operationalized by measuring the number of months in which the project had activity over its lifetime (from earliest to latest commit).
Theoretically, projects experiencing bursts of activity may prefer restrictive licenses to safeguard contributions made during active periods, particularly when development slows. 
This aligns with Fershtman and Gandal's~\cite{fershtman2007open} findings that restrictive licenses can help protect developers' interests.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis (H4)}: The number of commits, files, earliest/latest commit times, active months and burstiness affect the license choice, reflecting the project's development trajectory.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}

The literature on open source software licenses highlights the complexity and importance of licensing decisions within the software development ecosystem.
Recent studies have examined the dynamics of license selection across various programming languages, shedding light on how different developer communities approach license adoption and enforcement.
While foundational works established the basic principles, contemporary research emphasizes the unique challenges posed by the specific requirements of different languages and development environments.
Our contribution provides a data-driven analysis of these dynamics, offering a scalable framework to identify and evaluate license selection trends across programming languages.
This research fills a critical gap in understanding how language-specific factors affect license compliance, enabling developers to make more informed choices as they navigate the complexities of open source software licensing.
Specifically, our study contributions are as follows:

\subsubsection{Comprehensive Identification of Licenses} 

Most research, such as the work by Wu et al.~\cite{wu2024large} and Xu et al.~\cite{xu2023lidetector}, primarily depends on explicit license declarations found in metadata files.
Others, like Feng et al.~\cite{feng2019open}, apply static analysis on binaries to detect embedded license texts.
However, these methods may overlook licenses that are not clearly stated or are stored in unconventional directories.
In contrast, this study utilizes a dataset from Jahanshahi et al.~\cite{jahanshahi2024license}, which adopts a more comprehensive approach by scanning the entire OSS ecosystem for any files containing ``license'' in their filepath.
This method captures not only standard license files but also other files potentially holding licensing details, ensuring that no relevant license information is missed.

\subsubsection{Scale and Scope of Analysis} 

Previous works often limit their scope to specific platforms (e.g., GitHub), a few package manager environments (e.g., NPM), or types of licenses (e.g., OSI-approved licenses), lacking a comprehensive, large-scale approach to detecting and analyzing licenses.
Our study expands this scope by analyzing the entire open source landscape, ensuring a more comprehensive cross-platform understanding of licensing practices.
