\section{Results and Discussions}

\subsection{RQ1: What are the types and prevalence of licenses across the entire open source software landscape?}

\subsubsection{Results}

We first counted the total number of projects that had at least one occurrence of any license during their lifetime.
We then identified the top 20 most used licenses, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dis1}.

Out of the 131,171,379 projects indexed by WoC, 22,281,342 projects had at least one license committed to their project at some point in their lifetime.
However, when examining the latest status of these projects, this number drops to 20,110,256 projects.

When interpreting the numbers shown in these figures, it is important to note that the sum of these numbers exceeds the total number of projects with a license.
This discrepancy arises because some projects have multiple licenses and are therefore counted in several categories.

To calculate the retention rate for each license, we determined the number of projects that still had the license in their latest state and divided this number by the total number of projects that had ever used the license.
This retention rate indicates the proportion of projects that continue to use the license over time, and it is also displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:dis1}.

As shown in the figure, the MIT license is the most prominent, followed by the Apache 2.0 and ISC licenses.
Notably, the deprecated GPL 3 license exhibits the highest retention rate, likely due to its strong copyleft nature.
In contrast, the Creative Commons 3 license (CC-BY-3.0) shows the lowest retention rate among the top 20 most used licenses.

A key methodological difference between our study and Wu et al. lies in the treatment of data points.
Wu et al. considered each version of a package as a separate data point, resulting in 46.59 million data points from 3.47 million projects.
This approach could introduce bias by overrepresenting projects with many versions, potentially skewing observed trends in licensing.
Conversely, our study treated each project as a single data point, providing a more balanced view of the open source ecosystem without overemphasizing projects with frequent updates.

The focus of Wu et al. on only five package managers might explain the significant discrepancy in the percentage of projects without a license.
Their study reported that 21.12\% of projects lacked a license\footnote{
Since the paper provides these percentages by package manager, we calculated the overall percentage by taking a weighted average based on the number of projects per package manager.
}, whereas our study found a much higher percentage of 83.01\%.
On the other hand, Cui et al. reported that only 10.51\% of projects were unlicensed.
The difference with Wu et al. likely stems from the fact that the package managers they examined are associated with more mature and well-maintained projects, which are more likely to have defined licenses.
Additionally, Cui et al. filtered their data to include only projects with over 1,000 stars, which might explain why the percentage of unlicensed projects is so low.
In contrast, our broader dataset includes a wide variety of projects, many of which may be less mature or less formally governed, leading to a higher incidence of unlicensed projects.

The analysis of copyleft versus permissive licenses further reflects the differences in scope.
Wu et al.'s study found a dominance of permissive licenses, with much lower percentages for copyleft and weak copyleft licenses.
However, our study, covering all programming languages, reveals a more diverse licensing landscape, with a lower percentage of permissive licenses and higher percentages of copyleft licenses.
This suggests that by focusing on just five package managers, Wu et al.'s study might underrepresent broader licensing trends, particularly the prevalence of copyleft licenses across the global open source ecosystem.

\subsubsection{Discussions}

The analysis of OSS licensing reveals that 83\% of projects lack a formal license, posing significant legal risks.
This highlights the need for developers to prioritize licensing to protect their work and set clear terms of use.
Our findings challenge previous studies, which often underestimate the prevalence of unlicensed projects by focusing on mature ones, missing the broader risks of software reuse.

Even though many of these unlicensed projects may seem trivial, their public availability still creates legal ambiguity and poses a risk of license noncompliance, especially when combined with the lack of thorough legal review in many downstream projects.
Jahanshahi et al.~\cite{jahanshahi2024beyond} demonstrated that in the context of copy-based reuse, nearly 18\% of reused artifacts originated from very small projects\footnote{
Projects with no stars and fewer than 10 commits.
}, while large projects\footnote{
Projects with more than 10 stars and 100 commits.
} accounted for only 32\% of reused artifacts.
This highlights the importance of addressing the issue of unlicensed code within the OSS ecosystem.

Among projects that are licensed, the MIT license is the most prevalent, reflecting a community preference for permissive licenses that facilitate broad adoption and collaboration.
However, the continued use of copyleft licenses like the GPL demonstrates that many developers are committed to preserving the open nature of derivative works.
This indicates a need for the OSS community to balance the ease of adoption associated with permissive licenses with the ideological values upheld by copyleft licenses.

Figure~\ref{fig:dis3} shows that ``conditional-open'', ``weak-copyleft'', and ``Public-domain'' license types have the lowest
retention with approximately quarter of the projects that ever had such licence changing it
in their latest version. For comparison, only approximately 8\% of
of ``copyleft'' licences were changed, which is consistent with our theory
that ideology-related licence choice should be most ``sticky.''   
%These results reveals the overall trends and stability of various licenses in the open source software ecosystem, providing insights into how different licenses are maintained and preferred over time.
The strong retention rates of licenses such as the EPL, MIT, and GPL further suggest their effectiveness in ensuring long-term project sustainability.
Developers should take these factors into account when selecting licenses to ensure their projects are well-supported and maintain community engagement over time.

To address these implications, the OSS community should prioritize enhancing education and awareness among developers about the legal significance of licensing and how to select the appropriate license for their projects.
Furthermore, efforts should be made to develop and promote tools that streamline the licensing process, making it more accessible for developers, especially those new to the field, to apply licenses correctly.
While standardization offers advantages, it is equally important to preserve a variety of licensing options to accommodate the diverse needs and philosophies of different projects.
By focusing on these areas, the OSS community can strengthen the legal security, sustainability, and collaborative potential of open source projects.

\subsection{RQ2: How do the characteristics of a project influence the choice of its license?}

\subsubsection{Results}

To answer this question, we employ a multinomial regression model as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:reg}.
It is essential to check for correlations among predictors in a regression model, as multicollinearity—high correlation between predictors—can distort results.
Multicollinearity makes it difficult to assess each predictor's impact, leading to unstable coefficients and inflated standard errors, ultimately compromising the model's reliability and interpretability.
Addressing this issue improves the model's validity~\cite{dormann2013collinearity}.

We used a 0.6 correlation threshold, as is commonly used to manage multicollinearity while retaining key predictors.
Correlations above 0.6 indicate overlapping information, and removing such variables reduces multicollinearity without sacrificing significant variance.
This cutoff strikes a balance, preserving some natural correlations while ensuring predictor independence~\cite{vatcheva2016multicollinearity}.

The estimated coefficients for the regression model are shown in Table~\ref{tbl:model-l}.
Given that the response variable has multiple outcome levels, the model provides a distinct set of coefficients for each outcome level relative to a chosen base level.
This approach is typical in models such as multinomial logistic regression, where the base level acts as a reference category for comparing all other outcome levels.
Each set of coefficients indicates the influence of the predictor variables on the probability of the response variable assuming a specific outcome level relative to the base level.
In our model, the base level is defined as projects with ``no license'', so the coefficients reflect the impact of the predictors on each license type compared to having no license.
It is important to note that the selection of the base level is arbitrary and does not affect the estimated probabilities of each outcome level, as the relative probabilities between outcome levels remain constant regardless of the chosen base level~\cite{agresti2012categorical}.

WoC's MongoDB project database tracks file counts by language extension, allowing identification of a project's main language based on the most frequent extension, consistent with standard practices~\cite{vasilescu2015data}.
For this predictor, we employed sum contrasts, also known as effect coding, where each level of the predictor variable is compared to the overall mean of all levels.
This approach is particularly advantageous in models where the goal is to compare each category to the overall mean rather than to a specific reference category, as it offers a more symmetric interpretation of the effects.
In the sum contrast method, the sum of the coefficients for all levels, including the intercept, must equal zero.
As a result, the coefficient for one level (typically the last level, which is TypeScript in our model) is not estimated independently but is implicitly determined by the other coefficients to ensure that the overall model remains balanced and interpretable.

Furthermore, in interpreting the earliest and latest commit times, it is important to emphasize that the predictors used in the model represent the time elapsed since those commits.
This means that a higher value for the earliest commit indicates that the commit occurred further in the past (i.e., the project is older), and a lower value indicates a more recent commit.

To improve model interpretability, we used the \textit{emmeans} package in R to compute marginal probabilities across outcome variable levels~\cite{lenth2024emmeans}.
This allows us to assess predicted probabilities for each license type based on programming language, while controlling for other predictors.

For numerical predictors, we estimated marginal probabilities at key quantiles (25th, 50th, and 75th), capturing variability in predicted outcomes\footnote{
Exceptions were made for variables with skewed distributions, such as female authors and forks, where the 25th and 50th quantiles were both 0; here, we used 0, 1, 3 and 0, 2, 5, respectively.
For adoption delay, we used 1, 3, and 12 months.
}.

This method helps interpret complex models, such as multinomial logistic regression, by focusing on marginal probabilities instead of coefficients, making results clearer and more actionable~\cite{agresti2012categorical} (see Figure~\ref{fig:probs}).

After estimating marginal probabilities, we used the \textit{pairs} function from the \textit{emmeans} package for pairwise comparisons to identify significant differences between predictor levels and outcome categories.
We applied the Tukey method to control for family-wise error rates~\cite{hsu1996multiple,hothorn2008simultaneous}.

The analysis was conducted in two ways: comparing outcome probabilities within predictor levels (e.g., programming language) and comparing predictor levels within each outcome category.
This bidirectional approach allows for a thorough exploration of the interaction between predictors and outcomes.

% commits
As the number of commits increases from 18 to 961, the probability of adopting a conditional open license drops from 4.60\% to 2.42\%.
Conversely, the likelihood of selecting a copyleft license rises from 23.53\% to 26.01\%, and the probability of choosing a permissive license increases from 54.26\% to 60.09\%.
In contrast, the probability of selecting a public domain license falls sharply from 4.16\% to 1.50\%, and the likelihood of opting for a weak copyleft license decreases from 13.42\% to 9.94\% as commit numbers rise.

% files
As the number of files in a project increases from 114 to 18,456, the probability of adopting a conditional open license rises from 1.66\% to 6.55\%, and the likelihood of selecting a public domain license increases from 1.32\% to 4.53\%.
The probability of choosing a weak copyleft license also grows from 7.15\% to 18.20\%. 
In contrast, the probability of adopting a permissive license decreases from 65.72\% to 46.48\%.
The likelihood of choosing a copyleft license remains relatively stable, fluctuating slightly from 24.10\% to 24.22\%.

% authors
As the number of core authors increases from 1 to 4, the probability of adopting a conditional open license decreases slightly from 3.66\% to 3.20\%, and the likelihood of selecting a copyleft license drops marginally from 25.17\% to 24.90\%.
In contrast, the probability of choosing a permissive license rises slightly from 56.91\% to 57.91\%.
The likelihood of adopting public domain and weak copyleft licenses shows minimal changes, decreasing slightly from 2.65\% to 2.41\% and from 11.59\% to 11.55\%, respectively.

As the number of female authors increases from 0 to 3, the probability of adopting conditional open and public domain licenses rises slightly, from 3.08\% to 3.96\% and from 2.36\% to 2.75\%.
Meanwhile, the likelihood of selecting copyleft and permissive licenses decreases modestly, with copyleft dropping from 25.24\% to 24.31\% and permissive from 57.73\% to 57.38\%.
The probability of choosing a weak copyleft license remains almost unchanged.

% forks
As the number of forks increases from 0 to 5, the probability of adopting a conditional open license decreases from 3.61\% to 3.03\%, and the likelihood of selecting a copyleft license drops from 25.87\% to 24.00\%.
Similarly, the probability of adopting a public domain license decreases from 2.79\% to 2.16\%, and weak copyleft declines from 12.43\% to 10.68\%.
In contrast, the probability of choosing a permissive license rises from 55.26\% to 60.10\%, indicating a trend toward permissive licenses as forks increase.

% downstream
As the number of downstream projects increases from 1 to 98, the likelihood of choosing conditional open, public domain, and weak copyleft licenses slightly increases, suggesting that more downstream projects may encourage adopting licenses offering more control.
For instance, the probability of adopting a conditional open license rises from 3.05\% to 3.53\% and weak copyleft from 10.67\% to 12.25\%, possibly reflecting a desire for greater influence as code usage expands.
Conversely, the probability of selecting permissive and copyleft licenses decreases slightly, with permissive licenses dropping from 58.51\% to 56.98\% and copyleft from 25.43\% to 24.62\%.
This may suggest that as downstream projects grow, developers prefer licenses with more specific conditions.

% earliest/latest commit
As the time since the earliest commit increases, the likelihood of choosing conditional open and permissive licenses decreases, with conditional open dropping from 3.78\% to 2.83\% and permissive from 60.89\% to 53.70\%.
In contrast, the probability of adopting a copyleft license rises from 22.03\% to 28.60\%, suggesting older projects prefer copyleft licenses.
Weak copyleft also increases slightly from 10.82\% to 12.38\%, while public domain probabilities remain stable around 2.46\%.

As time since the latest commit increases, indicating longer inactivity, the likelihood of conditional open, copyleft, and weak copyleft licenses rises slightly—3.29\% to 3.39\%, 24.90\% to 25.15\%, and 11.41\% to 11.93\%, respectively.
Conversely, the probability of permissive and public domain licenses decreases, with permissive dropping from 57.87\% to 57.11\% and public domain from 2.50\% to 2.40\%.
This suggests that projects with more flexible licenses may either stay active longer or are less common among older, inactive projects.

The results show that as burstiness increases, the likelihood of adopting permissive licenses rises slightly from 57.18\% to 57.89\%.
This shift may reflect the fast-paced nature of bursty development, where developers prefer permissive licenses for their flexibility and ease of collaboration, facilitating quicker code integration and broader adoption.
In contrast, licenses with more conditions, like conditional open and copyleft, see minimal changes, likely because their complexity could slow down development in such environments.

% delay
As the adoption delay increases from 1 month to 12 months, the likelihood of choosing conditional open, public domain, and weak copyleft licenses rises slightly, possibly because longer delays give developers more time to consider legal and community implications, making protective licenses more appealing.
In contrast, the probability of adopting a permissive license decreases, suggesting the initial appeal of flexibility diminishes over time.
The copyleft license remains stable, indicating its adoption is less affected by the timing of decisions.

% language
The differences in licensing outcomes across various programming languages can be attributed to factors such as community culture, typical use cases, and historical influences associated with each language.
For example, languages like JavaScript, Ruby, TypeScript, and Go demonstrate a strong preference for permissive licenses, likely due to the open and collaborative nature of their development communities.
These languages are often used in web development and modern software projects, where permissive licenses like MIT or Apache are favored because they offer maximum flexibility.

In contrast, Perl and R show a stronger preference for copyleft licenses, reflecting community values centered on openness and sharing.
Perl's role in the open source movement and R’s academic use, where transparency is key, contribute to the popularity of copyleft licenses like GPL.
Similarly, C/C++ projects frequently adopt copyleft licenses due to their ties to the free software movement and critical system software, where open access is essential for security and collaboration.

Java, Python, and Go's dominance of permissive licenses is most likely driven by their widespread use in commercial and academic settings, where permissive licenses facilitate easy integration with proprietary solutions.
Developers choose these licenses to avoid legal issues and ensure broad adoption.

Public domain and conditional open licenses are less common but still noteworthy in languages like C/C++ and Go.
These licenses appeal to developers seeking either maximum user freedom or control over their software's use, though lower adoption suggests most developers prefer the protection offered by permissive or copyleft licenses.

In summary, software project licensing preferences are influenced by various factors, including the number of commits, files, core authors, female authors, forks, downstream projects, project age, and activity levels.
As commits increase, projects tend to favor copyleft and permissive licenses, while conditional open, public domain, and weak copyleft licenses become less common.
In contrast, projects with more files shift away from permissive licenses toward conditional open, public domain, and weak copyleft licenses, with copyleft licenses remaining stable.

The number of core authors slightly reduces the likelihood of adopting conditional open, copyleft, and public domain licenses, while permissive licenses gain a slight preference.
Projects with more female authors tend to adopt conditional open and public domain licenses more frequently, with a corresponding decline in copyleft and permissive licenses.

As forks increase, there is a clear preference for permissive licenses, while conditional open, copyleft, public domain, and weak copyleft licenses decrease.
Similarly, as downstream projects grow, developers lean toward licenses with more specific conditions for control and protection.

Over time, older and inactive projects shift from permissive and conditional licenses to copyleft and weak copyleft, while projects in bursty development environments slightly favor permissive licenses for their simplicity.

Ultimately, licensing choices are shaped by cultural, historical, and practical factors.
Permissive licenses dominate many programming languages due to their flexibility, while copyleft licenses are preferred in communities that prioritize long-term software freedom.
Language-specific use cases and historical context also play a key role in shaping these preferences.

\subsubsection{Discussions}

The analysis of how project characteristics affect license choices in open source software (OSS) projects offers important insights for developers and the broader OSS community.

Older projects tend to favor copyleft licenses, reflecting the early OSS movement's emphasis on preserving software freedom, while newer projects increasingly adopt permissive licenses, prioritizing flexibility and broad adoption.
This shift suggests that modern OSS development values ease of use and integration over strict control of derivative works.
The community should aim to balance historical ideals of software freedom with the demand for flexibility, helping developers make informed choices that align with project goals and values.

Active projects, particularly those showing recent and consistent activity, are more likely to adopt licenses, especially permissive ones.
This highlights a growing recognition of the need for legal clarity as projects mature and attract more contributors.

Smaller teams, especially those with fewer core authors, are more inclined to adopt licenses with specific conditions, such as conditional open licenses.
Projects with more female contributors also show a preference for these licenses as well as copyleft licenses, possibly to ensure protections.
The OSS community should provide resources to help smaller, diverse teams navigate licensing options that safeguard their collaborative efforts while aligning with their goals.

Programming language significantly influences license choices.
For example, C-based projects often prefer copyleft licenses, while Go and TypeScript projects lean toward permissive or conditional open licenses.
Developers should be aware of how their language community influences licensing norms, and the community could offer language-specific guidelines to align new projects with best licensing practices.

The trend suggests that as the number of downstream projects increases, developers may prefer licenses that offer more control, such as conditional open and weak copyleft licenses.
This shift likely reflects a desire to manage how their code is used and modified as it spreads across more projects.
On the other hand, the slight decline in permissive and copyleft license adoption may indicate that developers are less inclined to use licenses that either impose few restrictions or very strict ones when their code is reused more widely.
This shift implies that, with greater downstream adoption, there is a preference for balancing openness with specific conditions.

The trends suggest that as projects grow in complexity (with more files), developers increasingly prefer licenses that offer more control, like conditional open, public domain, and weak copyleft licenses.
This likely reflects a desire to protect their work as the stakes of misuse or modification rise with larger projects.
The decline in permissive licenses indicates that developers may view these as insufficient for larger, more complex codebases. 
Meanwhile, the stability of copyleft licenses suggests that developers choosing them may prioritize ideological commitments over project size.
These shifts could impact collaboration and reuse by imposing more conditions on larger projects.

Lastly, the shift toward permissive licenses as forks increase suggests that developers prioritize broader adoption and collaboration, but this comes at the cost of reduced control over the project's use and direction.
As more forks typically lead to greater participation, project maintainers may need to reassess their licensing strategies to ensure they align with their goals.
While permissive licenses encourage flexibility, establishing clear contribution guidelines and monitoring derivative works can help maintain some oversight, ensuring that the project’s original intent remains intact as it grows.
Balancing openness and control becomes crucial as the project expands through forks.

By addressing these insights and implications, the OSS community can foster a more legally secure, inclusive, and sustainable environment for open source development, encouraging developers to make informed and strategic decisions about licensing that align with both their project's needs and the broader community's values.
