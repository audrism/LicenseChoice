\section{Results and Discussion}

We organize our results in three parts: prevalence and retention of license 
types, the regression analysis of how license transitions relate to 
project performance, and the year-before/year-after robustness analysis
of license switches.

\subsection{Types and Prevalence of Licenses}

\subsubsection{Our Findings}

($17\%$) had committed at least one license file in their lifetime;
$20{,}110{,}256$ still had one at the latest state. Figure~\ref{fig:dis1}

\subsubsection{Comparison with Literature}

Table~\ref{tab:res1a} compares our no-license rate and license-type
mix against prior work (see also Table~\ref{tbl:litRev}). The
no-license fraction is the most striking: $83.01\%$ in our
$131$M-project sample vs.\ $21.12\%$ in \citet{wu2024large}
(weighted across 5 package managers, $3.47$M projects) and $10.51\%$
in \citet{cui2023empirical} ($\geq 1000$-star GitHub projects).
The four-fold gap is sample-selection-driven: package-manager
membership and stars both filter for mature projects with explicit
governance, of which fully licensed status is one indicator. Our broader
sample exposes a much larger long tail of unlicensed
repositories.

\subsubsection{Implications}

The 83\% no-license rate poses legal risk to downstream reusers and
discourages innovation by preventing actors who do attend to licensing
from using these projects.
Our findings challenge prior estimates, which underestimate
the prevalence of unlicensed projects by focusing on mature ones,
missing the broader risks of software reuse. Even though many of
these unlicensed projects may seem trivial and technically not OSS,
their public availability still creates legal ambiguity and poses a
risk of license noncompliance, especially when combined with the lack
of thorough legal review in many downstream projects.
The risk is not academic: \citet{jahanshahi2025beyond} report that
$\sim$18\% of reused artifacts originate from very small
projects.\footnote{No stars and fewer than 10 commits.}
MIT's dominance reflects a long-running preference for permissive
licenses; the continued use of copyleft licenses like GPL shows that a
meaningful subset of developers remain committed to preserving the
open nature of derivative works.

Examining the distribution of license type counts in projects
(Figure~\ref{fig:pie}), we observe that 12.5\% of the 22.3M projects
with at least one license held more than one license type during their
lifetime (8.0\% held two distinct types and 4.5\% held three or more).
Among the projects with multiple license types in their lifetime, some
still hold multiple types in their latest state (i.e., they did not
collapse to a single license) and others deleted all licenses. The
\emph{middle group}---projects that held multiple license types during
their lifetime but converged to a single license type at the latest
state---is the group we analyze license changes in. This middle group
yields the 23{,}619 projects that enter the regression.

The model produces a coefficient set per outcome. We use
sum contrasts for the language factor (each level compared to the
across-language mean) and include a Language $\times$ change-direction
interaction. In the sum-contrast convention the level
coefficients sum to zero, so the global R2P effect averages across
languages. R2P (restrictive$\rightarrow$permissive) is contrasted
against P2R (permissive$\rightarrow$restrictive) as the base.

Type III MANOVA tests (Pillai's trace) show all predictors
significantly improve model fit ($p \ll 0.001$).

% model performance
Model fit was evaluated using $R^2$ from univariate regressions. For the
primary AFvAL design, $R^2$ ranges from 0.13 to 0.45 across outcomes; for
the tighter BLvAL design it ranges from 0.03 to 0.06, as expected---bracketing
the switch with a one-year-before baseline differences out most of the
across-project variance, leaving less to explain. Both are modest but typical
for large-scale socio-technical data with high variability. Residuals showed
no irregular patterns. Multicollinearity was assessed via generalized variance
inflation factors (GVIFs); all predictors had adjusted
$\text{GVIF}^{1/(2\,\text{Df})}$ values below standard thresholds. In
particular, the two pre-treatment popularity proxies were near-orthogonal to
the rest of the model (adjusted GVIF of 1.15 and 1.16), and the highest value
was 2.40 (license-type adoption proportion), confirming that adding the
popularity controls did not introduce collinearity.

To better understand the effect of license change direction on project metrics,
we calculate the odds ratios for the change direction predictor and its 
interaction with language, considering only those predictors that are 
statistically significant at the 95\% confidence level. This ensures that 
the results focus on meaningful relationships rather than noise. 
Figure~\ref{fig:odds} displays these significant odds ratios along with 
their 95\% confidence 
intervals.\footnote{Please refer to the replication package for the full 
set of coefficients and p-values.} 
Since the analysis is based on a multivariate regression model,
the odds ratios are presented across different output variables, allowing
for a comparison of how the change direction predictor and 
its interaction with language influence various aspects of the model.

In these models, each coefficient represents a change in the
log-odds (or log-count) of the outcome. The main R2P effect
\(\beta_{\text{R2P}}\)
shows how the outcome shifts when moving from a restrictive to
a permissive license under sum contrasts (i.e., averaged across
language levels). A significant R2P:Language interaction
\(\beta_{\text{R2P:Lang}}\)
adds to (or subtracts from) the overall R2P coefficient for
that specific language. To obtain the total
restrictive\(\rightarrow\)permissive effect for a given language,
we sum \(\beta_{\text{R2P}} + \beta_{\text{R2P:Lang}}\)
and then exponentiate.

The per-metric numbers reported below are from the popularity-controlled
specification. Because pre-treatment popularity is included throughout,
the global R2P main effects are already adjusted for project popularity,
and the language-specific interactions---which are \emph{robust} across
both the AFvAL and BLvAL designs---form the primary narrative.

Figure~\ref{fig:odds} plots the significant odds ratios. The
language-specific contrast is the headline: \emph{C/C++ projects show
consistently \emph{reduced} growth} under
restrictive$\rightarrow$permissive transitions---fewer commits
(OR=0.69), files (0.76), and blobs (0.66), fewer authors (0.93), and
shorter sustained activity (0.85)---partially supporting H1a, H3a, H3b,
and H3c for that ecosystem. \emph{Python projects show the opposite},
with increased file (OR=1.46) and blob (1.24) growth and broader
upstream integration (1.22) under permissive licensing. PHP, Go, and
Ruby show smaller and mixed effects. After controlling for pre-treatment
project popularity, the \emph{global} R2P main effects on files
(OR=1.04), upstream (1.00), and downstream (1.05) are not significant:
once popularity is held fixed, license direction has no ecosystem-wide
effect on these metrics, and the robust associations are
language-specific rather than global. Two outcomes do retain a
significant global association in the controlled model: commits (OR=0.83)
and sustained activity (active months, OR=0.92) both decline modestly
under restrictive$\rightarrow$permissive transitions.

\subsubsection{Robustness}\label{sec:robustness}

We assess robustness along two axes: the choice of baseline window and the
inclusion of pre-treatment popularity controls. Table~\ref{tbl:robustness}
reports the main R2P (restrictive$\rightarrow$permissive) odds ratios under
both the AFvAL and BLvAL designs, alongside the language-specific effects.

\paragraph{Baseline window.} Our primary (AFvAL) specification compares the
year after the first license adoption with the year after the final
adoption. Because the first window often falls in a project's earliest
months, it could conflate license effects with project maturation. We
therefore re-estimate the model on the same 23{,}619 projects using the year
\emph{before} the final adoption as the baseline (BLvAL design), which
brackets the switch tightly. The language-ecosystem pattern persists across
both designs: C/C++ remains negative and Python positive. The significant
negative global associations with commits (OR=0.83 AFvAL, 0.84 BLvAL) and
sustained activity (active months, OR=0.92 in both designs) are stable
across the two baselines, confirming they are not an artifact of the
baseline window. Both effects clear a Bonferroni correction across the
seven outcomes ($\alpha/7\approx0.007$; commits $p<0.002$ AFvAL /
$p<0.002$ BLvAL, active months $p<0.001$ in both).
Figure~\ref{fig:odds-blval} shows the corresponding odds ratios; the
C/C++-negative and Python-positive interactions persist under this baseline.

\paragraph{Popularity controls.} A project's pre-treatment popularity could
confound the relationship between license direction and subsequent activity:
popular projects both attract more activity and may switch licenses
differently. We control for two pre-treatment popularity proxies measured in
the year after first adoption---upstream-project count (consumer-position
popularity) and downstream-project count (code-reuse popularity). The two are
not redundant (Spearman correlation 0.43) and are near-orthogonal to the
remaining predictors (adjusted GVIF 1.15 and 1.16). With popularity
controlled, license direction has no significant global effect on files,
upstream, or downstream project counts; the robust associations are
language-specific rather than global.

\subsubsection{Implications}

The analysis is observational and conditioned on the projects that
switched license types, so we report associations rather than
prescriptions. Two patterns are stable enough to inform downstream
tooling: (i) \emph{language ecosystem mediates the association} between
license direction and project dynamics far more than direction does on
its own (the C/C++-negative / Python-positive contrast holds across both
designs); license-recommender tools that ignore language ecosystem will
give systematically biased advice; (ii) once a project's pre-treatment
\emph{popularity} is controlled, the apparent global ``permissive helps''
effect on files, upstream, and downstream counts disappears, leaving only
modest negative associations with commits and sustained activity. These

A plausible mechanism for the C/C++ vs.\ Python contrast is community
composition: C/C++ projects that adopt copyleft early tend to attract
contributors motivated by strong-copyleft values, and relaxing that
constraint may erode that pool; Python projects, in contrast, sit in an
ecosystem dominated by permissive norms~\cite{lerner2005economics}, so
moving \emph{toward} permissive licenses aligns them with the ambient
norm and may lower a barrier to reuse and contribution. We flag this
as a candidate mechanism, not a tested claim.
