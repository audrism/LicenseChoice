\section{Conclusions}

We characterized the prevalence and types of licenses across more than
131 million open source projects and modeled how within-project license
transitions relate to subsequent project activity. Two findings emerge
clearly.
\textbf{(1)} 83\% of OSS projects lack a formal license, four times the
rate reported by prior studies that filtered on stars or
package-manager presence; this exposes a much larger long tail of
legally-ambiguous reusable code than was previously known, posing
compliance risk to downstream reusers and potentially inhibiting reuse
by actors who attend to licensing terms.
\textbf{(2)} For projects that switch license types, the apparent
global effects of switching to a permissive license on files,
upstream and downstream project counts collapse under popularity
controls and are not robust. What remains robust across designs is a
language-ecosystem-mediated pattern: C/C++ projects show reduced
activity after switching to permissive licenses, Python the opposite. A
year-before/year-after reanalysis on the same projects confirms that
these patterns are not an artifact of the baseline window: the
language-ecosystem contrast persists, as do modest negative
associations between restrictive$\rightarrow$permissive transitions and
both commits and sustained activity.

These results carry a concrete implication for tooling: because license
direction interacts so strongly with language ecosystem, license
recommenders and compliance tools that ignore a project's ecosystem
will give systematically biased advice. The small magnitude of the
remaining global effects further suggests that future work should
combine this regression with qualitative case studies in specific
language communities to understand the mechanisms behind the
C/C++ and Python contrast.
